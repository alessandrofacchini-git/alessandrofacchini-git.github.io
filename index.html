Hi, I’m Alessandro! I have a background in economics and public policy and experience in academic and policy-oriented research. My work has involved economic and econometric analysis, large-scale data, and interdisciplinary research across academic and institutional settings. I enjoy combining quantitative methods with a broader perspective on how individuals, institutions, and societies function, and I am motivated by research that contributes to a better understanding of real-world economic and social challenges.

Research Interests

My research interests span behavioral economics, decision-making, labor economics, and public policy. I am interested in using economic theory and quantitative methods to study how individuals and institutions make decisions and how these decisions shape economic outcomes and inequality. I combine econometric methodology with large-scale data to test economic theories and evaluate policy-relevant questions, with an emphasis on methods that rely on relatively few assumptions. My work includes both methodological research on revealed preference, rationality, and nonparametric inference, and applied research on labor markets, firms, inequality, and public policy.

Work in Progress

  • Nonparametric analysis of intertemporal choice and present bias.
    Why do people often act against their own plans—procrastinating on unpleasant tasks, failing to save for the future, or repeatedly choosing immediate gratification over larger future rewards? A central explanation in behavioral economics is present bias: the tendency to place disproportionate weight on immediate gratification. Yet identifying present bias from observed choices is difficult. The same pattern of behavior can arise from other sources, including uncertainty about the future, liquidity constraints, differences in the way costs and rewards are experienced, or simply noise in individual choices. This raises a fundamental question: how much of measured time-inconsistency reflects genuine self-control problems, and how much reflects other features of the decision environment? This chapter develops a nonparametric framework for answering this question. The approach combines economic theory, experimental variation, and econometric methods to test models of intertemporal choice while imposing relatively few assumptions on preferences and behavior. The empirical design uses convex time budgets over effort, allowing the same individuals to be observed from multiple temporal perspectives—for example, when deciding in advance how to allocate an unpleasant task across time and when making the choice again when the future becomes the present. Exogenous randomized interventions are then used to vary features of the environment that can otherwise masquerade as present bias, providing a way to distinguish genuine time-inconsistency from alternative explanations. A key methodological challenge is that standard revealed-preference tests are deterministic: when choices contain measurement error or random decision noise, they can incorrectly classify individuals as irrational. I therefore develop a noise-robust testing framework that evaluates whether observed choices can be rationalized by different intertemporal preference models without simply discarding observations that do not fit a deterministic model. The framework combines nonparametric bounds with latent-variable integration and conditional moment inequalities, allowing the data to distinguish between models while retaining uncertainty about unobserved components of behavior. Rather than imposing a particular functional form for preferences or treating a single estimated parameter as definitive evidence of present bias, the approach asks which behavioral models remain consistent with the observed choices and experimental variation. The broader aim is to rethink how present bias should be understood. In standard models, β is often treated as a stable individual characteristic: a person is either more or less present-biased. The framework developed here instead allows time-inconsistency to depend on the state in which decisions are made and on the domain of the choice. This distinction is important for understanding phenomena ranging from procrastination and self-control to saving, consumption, health behavior, and the use of digital technologies. If present bias is partly a response to the environment rather than a fixed trait, then both its measurement and its policy implications change fundamentally. Overall, the chapter provides a framework for studying intertemporal rationality as an empirical question rather than taking observed impatience as direct evidence of a behavioral bias. By combining revealed preference, experimental design, and modern econometric inference, it seeks to determine when apparent present bias represents genuine time-inconsistency, when it can be explained by alternative mechanisms, and how these distinctions can be used to better understand human decision-making.
