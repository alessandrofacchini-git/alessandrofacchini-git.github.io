Beyond the Trait

Present bias as a reaction function

Revealed preference, empirical evidence, and state-dependent self-control

Present bias is often estimated, reported, and calibrated as a personal parameter: a single β for each individual. This paper asks whether that number exists. The evidence suggests otherwise: β shifts with the state at the moment of choice, exhibits trait-like stability within but not across trade-off structures, and depends on the person-reward pairing rather than on either alone.

Alessandro Facchini

01The question

One number per person?

In the standard model of present bias, anything that does not happen now is discounted by an extra factor β ≤ 1, on top of ordinary discounting. With β = 1 there is no present bias; the lower β, the stronger the pull of the present.

β is often estimated as a personal parameter, carried across goods, states and settings. Yet published estimates of the “same” parameter run from 0.34 to 1.03 — a spread no single study’s confidence interval covers. Read as noise, that is imprecision. Read structurally, it is one number failing to capture a response that depends on context.

The central insight: β is better understood as a reaction function than a fixed personal trait. It varies with the state at the moment of choice and depends on the person--reward pairing within a given trade-off structure.

02The measuring stick

Present bias hides in plans and shows in actions

Asked days ahead how much to work on a future date, a person faces effort and pay that both lie in the future: β multiplies both and cancels. The plan reveals long-run preferences.

On the day itself the effort is immediate and only the pay is discounted by β, so the person does less than planned. The gap between the two first-order conditions is exactly log β. Every estimate in the paper starts from this plan-versus-act wedge.

In the paper: Section 2 and Section 3.2 ↗

Measuring in this coordinate has three advantages. It is scale-free: the gap in tasks grows with the size of the plan, the log-wedge does not. It is curvature-free once each comparison shares one elasticity. And it is robust to a classic transformation bias: averaging individual ratios \( \exp(\hat{\theta}/\hat{b}) \) with noisy denominators inflates β (in one dataset, the naive average of individual estimates is 1.38 against a corrected 0.92).

03The map

Every intertemporal choice sits on three axes

A single β must be the same along all three: the sign–timing structure of the trade-off, the good, and the state at the moment of choice.

The axes are independent, but existing literatures are not spread across them. Money studies sit almost entirely in reward–reward choices made in calm states; effort studies in cost–cost choices. Comparing βmoney with βeffort therefore changes the good and the structure at once. The three designs in the paper each move along specific axes while holding the others fixed.

04Method

Before the data: what can a test actually tell?

Letting β vary freely with the state explains any data, and so tests nothing. Empirical content comes from restricting how β varies.

The paper gives a revealed-preference characterization of state-dependent quasi-hyperbolic discounting, extending Echenique, Imai and Saito (2020), and orders the possible restrictions as a ladder. Each rung is more restrictive — and more testable — than the one above it.

In the paper: Sections 2.1–2.2 ↗

Why the usual statistic misleads

Heterogeneity in β is often assessed using level statistics, which address a different question: "Is the plan-versus-action wedge different from zero in this state?" A nonzero wedge reveals the presence of PB, but not whether it varies with the state. To test state dependence, I instead weight each decision by its assigned state minus its expected value under the experimental design, constructing a recentered instrument.

Recentering in one picture

In the design of Augenblick and Rabin (2019), what is randomized is the order of a session’s parts, not the state itself: a decision is “hot” if its block came after ten mandatory tasks. Because the session has a fixed structure, some decisions are hot far more often than others. Recentering subtracts each decision’s expected exposure μ, so that only the luck of the draw is left (Borusyak and Hull, 2023).

05Result one · the state

The state moves the act, not the plan

Augenblick and Rabin (2019) randomized whether each of 100 people made their choices before or after a block of ten mandatory transcription tasks. Crossing that assigned state with the vantage gives a 2 × 2 design within the same people, on a common wage grid.

Just after working, people cut the effort they choose for now; their plans for future work do not move. Present bias rises from β = 0.88 to 0.74. The study’s published, pooled β is a state-weighted average of the two.

In the paper: Section 5 ↗

Could it be chance?

Exact randomization inference keeps every choice fixed, redraws the session orderings 999 times within strata, and asks how often chance alone produces an effect as large as the one observed. No large-sample approximation and no model of the errors is needed.

Not projection bias. A model in which people project their current state onto the future would shift plans and acts alike. Here — on the original 72-subject sample — the effect is concentrated on choices executed minutes later (−0.141 log points) and vanishes for choices about work days ahead (+0.008); the difference is significant (p < .007, and .007 after recentering). Projection bias has no scope for immediate choices, where the state at choice is the state at execution.

06Result one, extended

Where β moves, and where it stops

Applying the same test across other dimensions, I find that β responds where the state is assigned within a person — the visceral state, the stakes, the closeness of the date. By contrast, patterns associated with differences across people largely fade once persistent individual heterogeneity is accounted for.

07Result two · the person

A trait within a structure, not across

If β were a personal trait, the same people would carry it from effort to money. In Augenblick, Niederle and Sprenger (2015) the same 101 people chose over both.

Individual estimates are noisy, so dispersion in them is partly sampling error. A bivariate measurement-error model separates the two, giving less weight to imprecise estimates. The result: genuine dispersion over effort, none over money. A parameter cannot be a variable in one setting and a constant in another — and no correlation can arise between a variable and a constant.

In the paper: Sections 3.3 and 6.1 ↗

Within one structure the picture flips. In Cheung et al. (2022), money, healthy snacks and unhealthy snacks are all traded off in reward–reward choices on one price grid. There, once noise is removed, the same people’s β’s are strongly correlated across rewards.

08Result three · the good

Money is the most present-biased reward

Cheung et al. (2022) asked 697 high-school students to allocate money, a healthy snack and an unhealthy snack over time — same prices, same delay, same delivery, decided in advance and again on the day.

In the paper: Section 6.2 ↗

A commodity taxonomy does not organize these data either. Within each food, students chose one of three snacks, and β varies across those six snack-choice groups by more than the whole money–food gap. Because the same students also chose over money, the spread can be decomposed.

Who carries the money gap? A general impatience trait should move every reward together — and the questionnaire’s own patience measure does exactly that. Students who drink or smoke look different: their β over money falls, while over food it barely moves. For low-income adolescents, cash is a general claim on immediate consumption that no single snack can satisfy.

Present bias attaches to the pair — this person, this reward — within a given trade-off structure and state, rather than to the person or the commodity alone.

09Magnitudes

Real, bounded, and confined to a minority

Rejecting the trait does not make the size of present bias meaningless. Within a setting, β still summarizes the weight on the immediate component, for the states, goods and people that setting covers. Two questions remain well defined: which values can be ruled out, and how many people are present-biased.

In the paper: Section 7.1 ↗

Field data tell a complementary story. In a Spanish household consumption panel, exact tests reject exponential discounting for most households — but recorded consumption is measured with error. The paper extends a measurement-error-robust test to quasi-hyperbolic discounting, centering the error within eight groups of goods each quarter so the test keeps its power.

Finally, how common is present bias? Using revealed and predicted choices over effort and money in Cerrone et al. (2026), the paper bounds the population share of each behavioural type, accounting for the sampling error of each measure.

10Welfare and policy

Most of the wedge is a surprise

When plan and act disagree, whose preferences count? Choices alone cannot say. The paper takes a deliberately conservative line that different normative views can share: only the part of the wedge that people fail to anticipate — the self-control gap Γ — is treated as a welfare loss.

A deviation someone sees coming (plan above act, and act equal to the forecast) is present bias, but a conflict between selves on which no welfare claim is made. This filter may miss some losses; its value is that it is not empty.

In the paper: Section 7.2 ↗

If people misjudge their future behaviour, can demand for commitment tell us who needs help? The paper splits each person’s forecast into the part that is accurate and the part that is wrong.

The error that defines the self-control gap also disables the remedy that would correct it.

1

Take-up does not screen need

Choosing commitment reveals who already knows they need it — who is already partly in control. Declining it is not evidence of a taste for flexibility.

2

Use instruments that do not rely on self-forecasts

Defaults, targets or schedules set by someone else, and incentives offered to everyone reach the gap. A target set by another person closes about 23% of it.

3

Design the environment

Part of the wedge is the decision context itself: timing, state, options. Employers, schools and platforms already shape it — leaving it unstructured is a choice too.

11Microfoundations

A model in which the state sets the weights

In the Multi-System Control framework, a far-sighted, slow and costly deliberative system and a fast, cue-triggered, myopic impulsive system value every option in parallel. The state — cues, visceral heat, fatigue, available control — does not change what each system wants. It changes how much each one weighs.

A fast arbitrator picks the action with the highest weighted value; a slow executive can raise the deliberative weight at a cost, and can also change the state of a future decision — which is what commitment is. Present bias is then a function of the state:

βeff(X) = ωD(X) / [ ωD(X) + ωI(X) · π ]

where ω are the weights of the two systems and π is how much more the impulsive system values the immediate part of the reward. Standard models are special cases.

In the paper: Section 8 ↗

The framework also reads the money results. Cash has no visceral appeal of its own; present bias over money reflects what it can buy now. Where cash buys no immediate consumption — as may be the case for the university subjects of Augenblick et al. (2015) — it predicts little present bias over money and little dispersion. Where cash opens access to visceral goods — as may be the case for the adolescents who drink or smoke — it predicts more present bias over cash than over food.

12Takeaways

One concept, many β’s

Within this paper’s data alone, the “same” parameter takes values from 0.74 to 0.99 — not as noise, but as the systematic response of a reaction function to states, structures, goods and people.

1

A conditional summary

A single estimate of β averages a state-dependent response over the states, goods and structures a dataset happens to sample.

2

Test the right restriction

State dependence as such is untestable. Level statistics detect present bias, not its dependence on the state; recentered functions of the assigned state do.

3

From the person to the environment

Most of the wedge is unforeseen and commitment demand misses it. Policy should look beyond individual targeting to the settings in which decisions are made.

Limits and next steps. The state result is also consistent with a transient rise in task costs after work; money always appeared in the same screen position in the three-good protocol; and existing datasets limit the range of states and goods. The paper proposes designs that separate these: a factorial randomization of a consumption cue across dated trade-offs over effort and money, randomized display positions with varying uses for cash, and repeated elicitations within one structure.

“Present bias is less a fixed property of who people are than a systematic response to the context in which they choose.”
